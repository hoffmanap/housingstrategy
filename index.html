How This Model Works

This tool estimates potential parcel-level redevelopment capacity under reformed zoning — it is not a prediction of what will get built, and it does not check whether a parcel's current base zoning allows the modeled yield. Each policy intervention is assumed to supply its own entitlement.

Eligibility

A parcel qualifies for redevelopment yield if it is assessor-coded vacant, or its building-to-lot coverage is below 15% (residential/current-use scenarios) or 45% (commercial & mixed-use scenarios), or it has no recorded units or building area.

How yield is calculated

Each active policy proposes a unit and/or job yield for a parcel. The model's headline number takes the single highest-yielding intervention per parcel, separately for units and jobs — it does not add multiple interventions together. Click any parcel on the map to see exactly which rule produced its yield.

With no policies checked and the default "Current Land Use Stays" scenario, the model shows zero net change citywide — this is a deliberate baseline, not an empty state. (An earlier version of this tool assigned a baseline job yield to underutilized commercial parcels even with nothing checked, which made "no intervention" misleadingly show large job totals. That's fixed: the "current use" scenario's job floor now only applies once at least one policy is active, so toggling everything off is a true do-nothing counterfactual to compare reforms against.)

Market Absorption Rate — and how we chose 8%

Gross capacity is multiplied by this adjustable rate to reflect that not every eligible parcel actually redevelops — most owners never sell or build even after a zoning change makes it possible. Worked example: if a policy combination makes 8,000 parcels eligible for an average of 2.5 net units each, that's 20,000 gross units at 100% absorption. At the default 8%, the model counts 1,600 of those as a realistic 10-year outcome — the rest remain technically possible but aren't assumed to get built.

The 8% default is the one number in this model grounded in real regional data rather than a generic rule of thumb: El Paso County averaged ~2,517 new housing permits/year from 2020–2024 (Census/FRED), down from ~3,100/yr (2013–2022) and ~4,300/yr (2003–2012). But most of that activity has been greenfield subdivision growth at the county's edge, not redevelopment of already-built-up parcels — the only category this model touches — so the true infill-specific pace is smaller still. 8% is set conservatively below the county-wide pace in that light. It's a defensible starting point, not a validated calibration — that would require comparing actual infill permits against this model's own eligible-parcel count, which the underlying data doesn't yet support. Full sourcing is in the README.

Why does eliminating parking minimums boost yield by 20% by default?

A parking space plus its drive aisle typically consumes 300–400 sq ft — often more land than the unit it serves. This is the central finding of decades of parking-policy research (notably UCLA's Donald Shoup, The High Cost of Free Parking): minimum parking requirements convert a large share of a site into space for cars, and removing that mandate frees land for housing or commercial space instead. That research explains why this matters; it doesn't hand over one universal conversion number, since actual land recovered depends on lot shape and each city's specific parking code. 20% is this model's own moderate, round-number default estimate of that boost — noticeable but not assuming a windfall. This number is adjustable — see "Model Assumptions (Advanced)" in the sidebar — with guidance on which direction to move it.

Where do the default 850 sq ft/unit and 1,000 sq ft/job figures come from?

These are general planning-literature benchmarks, not El Paso-specific data — no local dataset exists to calibrate them, unlike the absorption rate above. 850 sq ft approximates a modest, efficient 1–2 bedroom multifamily unit. 1,000 sq ft/job is a blended midpoint across commercial uses that actually vary widely — dense retail/office can run 200–500 sq ft/employee, while warehouse/light-industrial can run 1,000–2,000+ sq ft/employee. Using one moderate figure everywhere avoids assuming the densest possible use on every parcel, but it means parcels that would realistically become dense retail are likely undercounted on jobs, and ones that would become light-industrial are likely overcounted. Both figures are adjustable in "Model Assumptions (Advanced)" in the sidebar, each with guidance on which direction fits your expectations for what gets built.

Geographic constraints

Historic district protection, transit-corridor-only filtering, and recorded deed restrictions can each be toggled. Deed restrictions, when enabled, are applied as a final override after all other calculations — a single-family-only covenant caps a parcel at 1 unit and 0 jobs regardless of scenario or policy.

Key limitations

  • Estimates capacity, not predicted outcomes — land price, financing, owner intent, and permitting timelines are not modeled.
  • Missing lot size defaults to 6,000 sq ft; missing building area defaults to 0 sq ft, which tends to count undocumented parcels as redevelopment-eligible — treat totals in data-sparse areas with extra caution.
  • Deed-restriction coverage reflects recorded covenants in the dataset only — informal or unrecorded restrictions are not captured.
  • "Eliminate Parking Minimums" can still show job yield under the Residential Only scenario — this is intentional (parking reform is treated as an infrastructure policy that enables ground-floor commercial regardless of the dominant land use), not a bug.
  • District 1's source data has a known gap: its parcel file is currently missing existing-conditions data for every parcel (0 of 950), unlike every other district. Any parcel with zero data on every field shows a warning in its popup. District 1's totals should not be trusted until the source file is regenerated with the same data join the other districts have — see the README's "Known Data Quality Issues" section.
